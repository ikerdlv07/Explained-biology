Type 1 Diabetes

What is it?

Type 1 Diabetes is a chronic disease in which the body is unable to produce enough insulin or any at all. Insulin is responsible for taking glucose into the cells. If there is an absence, glucose remains in blood vessels, causing serious issues in the short and long-terms. Moreover, it is an autoimmune disease.

What is an autoimmune disease?

The immunity system recognizes and attacks strange agents, such as viruses and bacteria, yet the healthy cells and tissues remain intact. This is called tolerance towards oneself. An autoimmune disease develops when the mechanism ruins, and the immunity-cells attack the healthy cells, causing swallowing and destruction.

In this case, T Lymphocytes attack beta cells, the producer of the insulin. Once 60%-90% of the beta cells have been destroyed, it develops type 1 diabetes

Pancreas and insulin

Pancreas is a vital organ which accomplishes either exocrine (expulsing something by a tube) and endocrine (without tube, directly to the blood) functions. Its exocrine functions are responsible for discomposing aliments into tinier, to digest the carbohydrates, fats and proteins.

In contrast, we will pay more attention to the endocrine functions which involve hormones in blood to regulate glucose.

Pancreatic islets or islets of Langerhans are structures composed of highly compacted cells, is the producer of hormones as insulin and glucose. Furthermore, it is considered a mini organ since its compliance, with endocrine and support cells.

Main cells:

  • Alpha cells (α): glucagon
  • Beta cells (β): insulin
  • Delta cells (δ): somatostatin

    • Alpha cells are responsible for the production of glucagon. Glucagon is freed to mobilise the stored glycogen. In addition, after hours of fasting, to regulate the sugar levels, alpha cells produce glucagon which will command the liver to produce glucose.

    When we eat, the glucose levels rise; the beta cells notice this and automatically free insulin. The insulin adheres mainly to the muscular and adipose tissues. Acting as a "key" to give entrance to the glucose.

    Delta cells, on the other hand, are the ones who control the production of insulin and glucagon. Consequently, having hypoglycemia if more insulin than needed is releases or causing hyperglycemia if more glucagon than needed is releases.

    What happens with insulin then?

  • Muscle: boosts the storage of glycogen (stored glucose)
  • Liver: boosts the synthesis of glycogen and depresses glucose’ production
  • Adipose tissue: increases glucose’ entrance and favours the storage of energy

    • That is why insulin is considered a hormone that changes a cell's full metabolism.

    Why does TD1 develop?

    Most diabetes cases, approximately 95%, are due to a complex interaction between genetic and environmental facts. Environmental factors are not the reason why the disease commences. Nevertheless, they are likely responsible for maintaining it actively, speeding it up or delaying it.

    Genetic factors:

    The TD1 is strongly associated with a mutation in HLA (Human Leukocyte Antigen); it is a genes combination which takes charge of showing the antigens to the T Lymphocytes. This is an essential process to difference between our own body cells and foreign pathogens. Additionally, it is astonishingly fragile. Hence, the minimum mutation in it may destroy the mechanism. Consequently, creating an autoimmune response.

    Patients that express these altered genes are more likely to develop celiac disease. It is acknowledged that around 6% of diabetics are celiac, yet the number is estimated to be bigger since most patients who appear in diabetes are not diagnosed owing to absence of digestive systems or mild.
